Grammar — making predictions

Talking about the past in English isn't always straightforward; sometimes we discuss events that happened one after another, while at other times, one action was already underway when another interrupted it. We might also need to speak about something that occurred prior to another past event or even imagine a different past that never actually happened.

To express all of this correctly, English uses three main past tenses: the simple past, the past continuous, and the past perfect. Each serves a specific function, and knowing which one to use helps us tell a story clearly and convey the correct sequence of events.

Simple Past

We use the simple past when one thing happens, and then another thing happens right after it. The actions are in order, like a list.

• I backed up my computer, then I shut it down

More examples:

• I woke up, had breakfast, and left the house.

• She opened the door and turned on the lights.

Past Continuous

We use the past continuous for a longer action that was happening in the past. Then, a shorter action (in simple past) interrupts it. • I realized someone was using my bank account when I saw $200 mysteriously disappear

Structure: was/were + verb-ing (long action) + simple past (short action that interrupts)

More examples:

• I was watching TV when the phone rang.

• They were walking home when it started to rain.

Past Perfect

We use the past perfect to show that something happened before another past event. It shows the first action in a sequence of two past actions.

• I didn't buy those things. Someone had copied my card. Structure: had + past participle

More examples:

• When I arrived at the station, the train had already left.

• She was tired because she had worked all night.

Past Perfect

We also use the past perfect when we report (retell) what someone said. If the person originally used the simple past or the present perfect, we change it to past perfect in reported speech.

• He asked me whether I had ever been the victim of an online crime.

(Original question: "Have you ever been the victim of an online crime?")

More examples:

• She said she had finished the project. (Original: "I have finished the project.")

• He told me he had seen that movie. (Original: "I saw that movie.")

Past Perfect

We use the past perfect to imagine a different past — something that didn't really happen. This is often used with "if" to talk about regrets or imaginary results.

• If the bank hadn't blocked my card so quickly, I would have lost it all!

Structure: If + past perfect, ... would have + past participle

More examples:

• If I had studied more, I would have passed the exam.

• If she hadn't missed the bus, she wouldn't have been late.
